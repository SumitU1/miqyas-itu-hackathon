# Global examples for gaps G1, G2, G3, G7 (sourced 31 Oct 2026)

Companion to `docs/POLICY_GAPS_CANDIDATES.md`. G4, G5, G6 and G8 are in `docs/GLOBAL_EXAMPLES_G4_G8.md`. Every item below was seen in a primary-source page returned by a search on 31 Oct 2026; quotes are short and exact as the page showed them. Not legal advice; not reviewed by a lawyer. "Seen in search excerpt" means the page text was returned by the search tool but the page was not opened separately.

## G1. Health data held by an app that no health-sector law covers
- **Example:** United States, Federal Trade Commission, Health Breach Notification Rule, 16 CFR Part 318, amended by a final rule published 30 May 2024 (89 FR 47054), in force 29 July 2024.
- **What it does:** the rule reaches "vendors of personal health records" that are not HIPAA-covered entities or their business associates, and the FTC says the 2024 changes make clear that health apps and connected devices are covered. A product counts as a personal health record if it "has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources" (FTC business blog, 25 Apr 2024).
- **Why it fits G1:** it closes the gap between a health-sector law (HIPAA) and a consumer app by naming the app category and the data it holds, so the consumer app has a regime and a regulator.
- **Sources:** https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-318 ; https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-changes-health-breach-notification-rule ; https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/30/2024-10855/health-breach-notification-rule
- **Confidence:** seen in search excerpts of the eCFR, Federal Register and FTC pages.

## G2. Does explicit consent legitimise a cross-border transfer of sensitive data?
- **Example:** European Union, GDPR Article 49(1)(a) (explicit consent to the proposed transfer, after being told the risks of no adequacy decision or safeguards) and EDPB Guidelines 2/2018 on Article 49 derogations, adopted 25 May 2018.
- **What it does:** the guidelines say derogations "must be interpreted restrictively so that the exception does not become the rule", tell exporters to use the standard transfer tools first, and say the GDPR "sets a high threshold" for the consent derogation. Note: the guidelines' "occasional" test is attached to Article 49(1)(b), (c) and (e), not to consent, so do not describe consent transfers as limited to occasional ones.
- **Why it fits G2:** another regime gives a published answer to "is consent alone enough", namely that it is a narrow, last-resort route.
- **Sources:** https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_2_2018_derogations_en.pdf ; https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML?uri=CELEX%3A02016R0679-20160504
- **Confidence:** seen in search excerpts of the EDPB PDF and the EUR-Lex consolidated text.

## G3. Is a consumer wearable a medical device? A published line
- **Example:** United States, FDA guidance "General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices", issued 6 January 2026, superseding the version of 27 September 2019.
- **What it does:** sets criteria (wellness intended use, low risk, non-invasive) and, since the 2026 update, says FDA "may" treat non-invasive wearables that estimate physiologic parameters such as blood pressure or heart rate variability as general wellness products if outputs are for wellness only (law-firm summaries of the guidance; the guidance PDF is the source). It adds worked examples of products that are, and are not, devices. The 17 June 2026 FDA close-out letter to WHOOP (reported by a law firm, Spencer Fane, 23 Oct 2026) shows the line being applied to a real product.
- **Why it fits G3:** the UAE ministry summary names "simple medical devices" with no definition in the English text we hold; the FDA text shows what a published threshold and examples look like.
- **Sources:** https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download ; https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices ; CRS note https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12657
- **Confidence:** guidance page and PDF seen in search excerpts; WHOOP close-out seen only in a law-firm article, so do not cite it as an FDA fact unless the FDA letter is opened.

## G7. What does "human review" of an automated decision have to look like?
- **Examples (two, complementary):**
  1. EU GDPR Article 22(3) lists safeguards "at least the right to obtain human intervention", to express a view and to contest the decision. The Court of Justice judgment of 7 December 2023, Case C-634/21 SCHUFA Holding (Scoring), read "decision" broadly, so a score that a third party relies on can itself be a decision.
  2. EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) Article 14 on human oversight of high-risk systems: the overseer must be able to understand the system's limits, stay aware of "automation bias", interpret the output, and "disregard, override or reverse" it, and may "stop" the system. One third-party page (artificialintelligenceact.eu) shows the Annex III high-risk obligations applying from 2 December 2027, and the Commission's service-desk page points to a "Digital Omnibus" FAQ. I have not confirmed the current application date, so the report must not state one without checking EUR-Lex.
- **Why it fits G7:** the UAE text asks that a human element be included on request but sets no standard; these two texts show a right (GDPR) and a design standard (AI Act) side by side.
- **Sources:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX%3A62021CJ0634 ; https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX%3A02016R0679-20160504 ; https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-14
- **Confidence:** seen in search excerpts of EUR-Lex and the Commission's AI Act Service Desk.
